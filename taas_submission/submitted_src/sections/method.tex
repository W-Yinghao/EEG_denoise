\section{Method: Subject-Aware Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model}
\label{sec:method}

We propose the Subject-Aware Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (\saddpm{}), which introduces subject-level adaptation into the learned reverse
process of a diffusion model for multi-subject EEG denoising. Importantly, \saddpm{} keeps the forward diffusion process subject-independent and injects
subject information only into the learned reverse denoising process. The subject embedding therefore does not change the Gaussian corruption schedule; instead, it modulates the trajectory by which the model estimates and removes noise. This separation preserves the stability of standard DDPM training while allowing the reverse process to adapt to subject-dependent signal morphology and artifact patterns. The model comprises three core components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \emph{standard, subject-independent forward diffusion process} that constructs noisy training states from EEG segments;
  \item a \emph{subject-conditioned reverse denoising process} in which multi-layer embedding injection adapts the denoising trajectory to a subject domain; and
  \item a \emph{training and transductive adaptation scheme} that optimizes the backbone and source-subject embeddings during training and estimates a new target-subject embedding from unlabeled target EEG at deployment.
\end{enumerate}

A standard DDPM assumes that the training data are drawn from a single distribution and does not explicitly model the distribution differences between subjects, which limits its denoising performance and transferability in practical cross-subject EEG applications. Different subjects differ in the spatial distribution, spectral characteristics, and contamination patterns of
their EEG; in multi-subject datasets and mobile-acquisition settings, electrode contact, background noise, and physiological state all vary, so a static, uniformly parameterized denoising strategy is difficult to keep effective. By adding a subject-aware mechanism on top of the DDPM, \saddpm{} systematically addresses this cross-subject domain shift while retaining the stability of diffusion modeling, yielding more robust denoising.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:method_problem}

We consider $N$ source subjects (domains) indexed by
$s\in\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,N\}$. For subject $s$, EEG segments $\mathbf{x}_0\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times L}$ ($C$ channels, $L$ time samples) follow a
subject-specific distribution $\mathcal{D}_s$, and the population distribution is the mixture $\mathcal{D}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_s\mathcal{D}_s$ with mixing weights $\pi_s\ge 0$ and $\sum_s\pi_s=1$. In paired denoising experiments,
$\mathbf{x}_0$ denotes the artifact-free target signal. In naturally recorded BCI-IV-2a data, where no artifact-free ground truth is available,
$\mathbf{x}_0$ denotes the preprocessed EEG segment used as the empirical target of the diffusion objective rather than a physiologically clean ground truth. A forward diffusion process (Section~\ref{sec:method_diffusion}) corrupts
$\mathbf{x}_0$ into a noisy state $\mathbf{x}_t$ at step $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ by injecting Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, and a
denoiser $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}$ is trained to predict $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, optionally conditioned on a subject embedding
$\mathbf{e}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.

For a predictor $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}$, the per-subject and population denoising risks are
\begin{equation}
  R_s(\theta,\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{x}_0\sim\mathcal{D}_s,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\big[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\,\cdot\,)\|^2\big],
  \qquad
  R(\theta,\phi)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_s\,R_s(\theta,\phi),
  \label{eq:risks}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.
The conditioning argument ``$\cdot$'' is empty for a \emph{subject-agnostic} model $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)$ and equals $\mathbf{e}(s)$ for the \emph{subject-aware} model
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s))$. Because $\mathcal{D}_s\neq\mathcal{D}_{s'}$ in general (Section~\ref{sec:related_subject}), a model fit on source subjects faces a distribution shift when applied to a target subject $s^\star$. In this paper we use a \emph{transductive subject-adaptation} setting: before denoising a target
subject, the model may access unlabeled EEG segments from that subject to estimate a target embedding $\mathbf{e}^\star$, but it does not use target task labels or artifact annotations. The quantities of interest are therefore the adapted target risk $R_{s^\star}(\theta,\phi,\mathbf{e}^\star)$ and the resulting downstream classification accuracy. The goal of \saddpm{} is to learn the shared parameters $(\theta,\phi)$ and source embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}(s)\}$ that minimize the population risk~\eqref{eq:risks}, while enabling rapid unlabeled adaptation of $\mathbf{e}^\star$ for a shifted target subject. \subsection{Forward and Reverse Diffusion Processes}
\label{sec:method_diffusion}

In \saddpm{}, EEG denoising follows the forward and reverse diffusion principles of the standard DDPM, but subject information is introduced only in the learned reverse denoising process. The model contains a forward diffusion process, which
simulates the progressive injection of Gaussian noise, and a reverse denoising process, which simulates the progressive recovery of the signal. The forward process is shared by all subjects; subject-encoding information modulates the
reverse network at each denoising step.

\paragraph{Forward diffusion.}
The forward process gradually transforms a target signal $\mathbf{x}_0$ into noise $\mathbf{x}_T$ that is approximately isotropic Gaussian. It is realized as a sequence of small Gaussian perturbations:
\begin{equation}
  q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid \mathbf{x}_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}),
  \label{eq:forward_chain}
\end{equation}
with each step defined as
\begin{equation}
  q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\,\beta_t\mathbf{I}\right),
  \label{eq:forward_step}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_t$ is the noise-variance hyperparameter at step $t$, which is increased slowly according to a predefined schedule so that the signal gradually loses its original structure. To simplify sampling, the DDPM allows the intermediate state at an arbitrary step $t$ to be sampled directly from
$\mathbf{x}_0$:
\begin{equation}
  q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\right),
  \qquad
  \alpha_t:=1-\beta_t,\quad\bar{\alpha}_t:=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s.
  \label{eq:forward_marginal}
\end{equation}
In \saddpm{}, the forward process is kept identical to that of the standard DDPM, with no additional modification, in order to preserve the stability and
theoretical consistency of the noise-injection process.

\paragraph{Reverse denoising.}
The reverse process learns a generative trajectory that recovers the target signal
from Gaussian noise, modeled as a parameterized Markov chain:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{e})
  =p(\mathbf{x}_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}
  p_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid \mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{e}),
  \label{eq:reverse_chain}
\end{equation}
where each reverse conditional is Gaussian and is conditioned on the subject
embedding through the learned reverse model:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid \mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{e})
  =\mathcal{N}\!\left(
  \mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,
  \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}),\,
  \sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}
  \right).
  \label{eq:reverse_step}
\end{equation}
Following the standard DDPM, the reverse variance is fixed to the noise schedule
($\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$). The mean is reparameterized through the combined
dual-decoder noise estimate:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e})
  =\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(
  \mathbf{x}_t-
  \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,
  \widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e})
  \right),
  \label{eq:reverse_mean_combined}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}$ is defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:combined_epsilon}. Unlike a subject-agnostic DDPM, \saddpm{}
conditions the reverse mean through the individual residual branch: at each step
$t$, $\mathbf{e}$ and the current state $\mathbf{x}_t$ determine a
subject-specific correction to the subject-invariant content prediction. This
conditioning lets the reverse denoising process adapt to subject-dependent signal
morphology and artifact characteristics without modifying the forward diffusion
schedule.

\paragraph{Trial-wise restoration from an observed EEG segment.}
The reverse chain in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reverse_chain} can generate subject-consistent EEG from Gaussian noise, but the downstream BCI experiment requires a
trial-wise denoised version of an observed segment. Given a preprocessed observed EEG window $\mathbf{y}$ and a subject embedding $\mathbf{e}$, \saddpm{} performs partial noising followed by reverse refinement. Specifically, for a denoising
strength $t_0\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ and $K$ Monte Carlo samples, we initialize
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{t_0}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t_0}}\,\mathbf{y}
  +\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_0}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(k)},
  \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
  \label{eq:partial_noising}
\end{equation}
and run the learned reverse process from $t_0$ to $0$ while conditioning on $\mathbf{e}$. The trial-wise denoised output is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}_0.
  \label{eq:trialwise_output}
\end{equation}
The parameter $t_0$ controls the restoration strength: a larger $t_0$ allows stronger artifact suppression but increases the risk of over-smoothing or altering task-relevant EEG structure. This inference rule distinguishes trial-wise denoising from unconditional generation and is the procedure used when SADDPM-denoised segments are passed to the downstream classifier.

\subsection{Subject Embedding and Conditional Guidance}
\label{sec:method_embedding}

The subject embedding $\mathbf{e}$ provides subject-domain information that guides the reverse denoising process. Two encoding strategies are possible:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{One-hot encoding}: each subject corresponds to a unique one-hot
  vector, suitable when the number of subjects is small.
  \item \textbf{Learnable embedding}: a trainable low-dimensional vector is
  learned jointly with the main network, automatically capturing latent
  inter-subject differences.
\end{enumerate}
In practice, we use learnable embeddings for source subjects. The embedding
$\mathbf{e}$ is supplied to the subject-conditioned residual path rather than to
the subject-invariant content predictor. It is injected at multiple levels of the
individual-difference branch to strengthen conditional control. This mechanism
enables \saddpm{} to recover the common structure of EEG through the content
branch while tailoring the residual denoising strategy to a subject domain,
improving recovery fidelity and cross-subject stability.

\paragraph{Transductive target-subject adaptation.}
For a target subject $s^\star$ not used to fit the shared backbone, a learnable
embedding cannot be assumed to exist a priori. We therefore use a transductive
adaptation protocol. Given an unlabeled target set
$\mathcal{U}_{s^\star}=\{\mathbf{y}_i\}_{i=1}^{M}$, we introduce a new embedding
$\mathbf{e}^\star$ initialized by the mean source embedding
$\bar{\mathbf{e}}=N^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{e}(s)$. The backbone and
source embeddings are frozen, and only $\mathbf{e}^\star$ is optimized with the
self-supervised diffusion loss
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{e}^\star}\; 
  \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{U}_{s^\star},\,t,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
  \left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-
  \left(
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{c}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
  +\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}^\star)
  \right)\right\|^2\right]
  +\lambda_e\|\mathbf{e}^\star-\bar{\mathbf{e}}\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:target_embedding_adapt}
\end{equation}
where
$\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{y}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.
This adaptation uses the target subject's unlabeled EEG distribution but does
not use motor-imagery class labels, clean targets, or artifact annotations. The
auxiliary subject-discrimination loss $\mathcal{L}_a$ is not applied during this
step, because the target subject is not a source class of the classifier head.
The procedure therefore resolves the embedding issue for target subjects while
preserving a label-free deployment setting. After adaptation, trial-wise
denoising uses $\mathbf{e}^\star$ in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:partial_noising}--\eqref{eq:trialwise_output}.

\subsection{Network Architecture}
\label{sec:method_architecture}

\subsubsection{Backbone Network}
\saddpm{} adopts an improved one-dimensional convolutional U-Net (1D~Conv
U-Net) to accommodate the temporal nature of EEG, using symmetric encoder and
decoder modules for multi-scale feature extraction and reconstruction. The
overall architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Encoder.} A stack of 1D convolutional blocks, each containing a
  convolution layer, group normalization, and a nonlinear activation (Swish).
  After each block the feature length is halved and the number of channels is
  increased, extracting local temporal features at progressively coarser scales.
  \item \textbf{Middle block.} Several self-attention blocks at the U-Net
  bottleneck capture global dependencies and strengthen long-range temporal
  modeling, which is important for cross-channel and cross-segment EEG patterns.
  \item \textbf{Decoder.} Symmetric to the encoder, built from up-sampling
  (transposed convolution or interpolation followed by convolution) blocks that
  progressively restore the temporal resolution. Skip connections pass
  intermediate encoder features directly to the corresponding decoder layers,
  preserving fine-grained local information and improving reconstruction
  quality.
  \item \textbf{Time embedding.} At each diffusion step $t$, a sinusoidal
  positional encoding embeds the step index and is injected into the
  convolutional blocks, making the network aware of the current denoising stage
  so that it can adjust its strategy accordingly.
\end{itemize}

On top of this shared backbone, \saddpm{} uses a dual-decoder design for
individual-aware modeling (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The shared encoder
maps the noisy state $\mathbf{x}_t$ to a latent representation, from which two
parallel decoders branch: a \emph{denoised-signal} (content) decoder that models
the subject-invariant target/content component, and an
\emph{individual-difference} decoder that uses the subject embedding to model the
subject-conditioned residual component. The reverse-step estimate is obtained by
combining the two branches, while a lightweight classifier head on the subject
branch encourages the individual-difference representation to encode
subject-discriminative signal information. The design is motivated by the
observation that EEG distributions differ significantly across subjects: a
single unconditional denoiser may either underfit subject-specific artifacts or
absorb them into the main content pathway. The dual-decoder structure therefore
routes population-level EEG morphology through the content branch and routes
subject-dependent corrections through the individual branch, yielding a reverse
denoising step that is adaptive without making the whole predictor
subject-specific.

Concretely, the denoised-signal (content) branch, parameterized by $\theta$,
predicts a subject-invariant noise component
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{c}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)$, whereas the
individual-difference branch, parameterized by $\phi$, predicts a
subject-conditioned residual component
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s))$. The combined
noise estimate used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reverse_mean_combined} is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}
  (\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s))
  =
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{c}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
  +
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s)).
  \label{eq:combined_epsilon}
\end{equation}
A classifier head $\omega$ reads the subject-branch representation and outputs
subject logits for the auxiliary subject-discrimination loss. This definition
aligns the architecture with the disentanglement objective: the content branch
models common EEG structure, while the individual branch carries the
subject-conditioned residual needed for subject-specific denoising.

\subsubsection{Subject-Aware Conditioning}
The subject embedding is fused into the network through conditional injection so
that feature processing and generation adapt to each subject. The mechanism has
three elements.

\paragraph{Embedding generation.} Each source subject label $s$ is mapped to a
trainable low-dimensional embedding $\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,
optimized jointly with the backbone parameters; we use $s$ and $\mathbf{e}(s)$
interchangeably as the conditioning argument when the embedding is available.
The embeddings are randomly initialized and refined in a data-driven manner,
capturing subject-level differences relevant to denoising.

\paragraph{Injection sites.} To preserve the content/residual decomposition,
the subject embedding is injected into the individual-difference path at multiple
levels. Before the convolutional layers of the residual decoder (and the
bottleneck features routed to that decoder), a linear transformation maps
$\mathbf{e}$ to the feature-map size, and the result is added to, concatenated
with, or used to modulate the residual features.

\paragraph{Fusion via feature modulation.} To balance training stability and
expressiveness, we use feature-wise modulation (FiLM): scale and shift
parameters derived from the subject embedding adjust the output distribution of
each convolutional block,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}'=\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\mathbf{e})\odot\mathbf{h}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(\mathbf{e}),
  \label{eq:film}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}$ is an intermediate feature, $\odot$ is element-wise
multiplication, and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\mathbf{e})$ (scale) and
$\boldsymbol{\delta}(\mathbf{e})$ (shift) are the modulation parameters produced
from the subject embedding by a small multilayer perceptron (MLP). The shift is
denoted $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ to avoid confusion with the noise-schedule
parameter $\beta_t$.

\subsubsection{Loss Function and Training Objective}
\label{sec:method_loss}
\saddpm{} adopts the same noise-prediction principle as the standard DDPM\@. For
a generic conditional DDPM denoiser $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\psi}$, the simplified
objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}(\psi)=
  \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbf{e}}\!\left[
  \left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\psi}\!\left(
  \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+
  \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,t,\,\mathbf{e}
  \right)\right\|^{2}\right],
  \label{eq:lsimple}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{e}$ denotes optional conditioning. In \saddpm{}, this generic
predictor is instantiated by the combined dual-decoder estimate in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:combined_epsilon}. At each step, the diffusion index $t$ is sampled
uniformly and the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ from a standard normal; the
backbone, branch parameters, and source-subject embeddings are optimized jointly.

To realize the dual-decoder, subject-aware design, the overall training
objective combines three terms (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}):
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}=\lambda_r\,\mathcal{L}_r+\lambda_o\,\mathcal{L}_o+\lambda_a\,\mathcal{L}_a,
  \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathcal{L}_r$ is the \emph{reverse (reconstruction) loss}, the
  noise-prediction loss applied to the combined two-branch estimate
  (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lr}), which drives accurate denoising;
  \item $\mathcal{L}_o$ is the \emph{orthogonality loss}, which decorrelates the
  representations of the denoised-content branch and the individual-difference
  branch, preventing the subject information from being absorbed or ignored by
  the main branch and keeping the two factors disentangled; and
  \item $\mathcal{L}_a$ is the \emph{subject-discrimination loss}, an
  angular-margin (ArcFace-style) classification loss on the subject branch that
  encourages the individual encoder to be discriminative of subject-level signal structure,
  using the subject label as supervision.
\end{itemize}
Concretely, let $\mathbf{Z}^{c},\mathbf{Z}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$ stack
the column-wise mean-centered content and subject features of a training batch of
size $B$, and let $y\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ be the ground-truth subject of a sample
among $N$ subjects (the $\ell_2$/angular normalization in $\mathcal{L}_a$ applies
to the classifier features, not to $\mathbf{Z}^{c},\mathbf{Z}^{s}$). The three terms are
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_r
  &= \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s}\!\left[
  \left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-
  \widehat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta,\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s))
  \right\|^{2}\right] \notag\\
  &= \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s}\!\left[
  \left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-
  \left(
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{c}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
  +
  \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\mathbf{e}(s))
  \right)
  \right\|^{2}\right],\label{eq:lr}\\
  \mathcal{L}_o &= \big\|\,{\mathbf{Z}^{c}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}^{s}\,\big\|_F^{2},\label{eq:lo}\\
  \mathcal{L}_a &= -\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\frac{e^{\,\kappa\cos(\vartheta_{y}+m)}}{e^{\,\kappa\cos(\vartheta_{y}+m)}+\sum_{j\neq y}e^{\,\kappa\cos\vartheta_{j}}}\right],\label{eq:la}
\end{align}
where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm, $\vartheta_j$ is the angle between the
normalized subject feature and the class-$j$ weight, $m$ is the angular margin,
and $\kappa$ is the ArcFace scale (named $\kappa$ to avoid clashing with the
subject label $s$). The reconstruction term $\mathcal{L}_r$ corresponds to the
$\oplus$ combination of the subject-invariant content prediction and the
subject-conditioned residual prediction in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}. If the
residual branch outputs zero, the objective reduces to a content-only
subject-agnostic DDPM; if the residual branch is active, the subject embedding
can correct the common denoising trajectory for subject-specific artifacts.
$\mathcal{L}_o$ decorrelates the content and subject representations; and
$\mathcal{L}_a$ supervises the classifier head $\omega$. The non-negative weights
$\lambda_r,\lambda_o,\lambda_a$ balance the three terms. Using noise prediction as
the primary objective stabilizes training and encourages progressive recovery of
the target signal across noise levels, while the orthogonality and
subject-discrimination terms jointly enhance cross-subject denoising and fidelity.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:method_theory}

We collect a few short, design-justifying results. In the main text we keep the
two that speak directly to the subject-aware design---\emph{when}
subject-conditioning helps (Prop.~\ref{prop:cond}) and what the orthogonality loss
enforces (Prop.~\ref{prop:orth})---together with the domain-adaptation view that
motivates the content/residual split (Prop.~\ref{prop:da}). Two standard facts
that we use but do not claim as contributions---that the subject-aware hypothesis
class contains the plain DDPM (Prop.~\ref{prop:contain}), and that the
noise-prediction loss controls the reverse-step reconstruction error via an exact
affine identity (Prop.~\ref{prop:prop})---are stated and proved in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} to keep the main text focused. All results
\emph{motivate} the architecture rather than guarantee performance; proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Unless stated otherwise, expectations are over the
data-generating process of Section~\ref{sec:method_problem}, and all quantities
are assumed to have finite second moments.

\begin{proposition}[Subject-conditioning and denoising risk]
\label{prop:cond}
Fix a diffusion step $t$ and consider the squared-error noise-prediction
objective of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lsimple}. Let $S$ be the random subject, let
$g^\star(\mathbf{x}_t,t)=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,t]$ be
the optimal subject-agnostic predictor with risk $R_u=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-g^\star\|^2$,
and let $h^\star(\mathbf{x}_t,t,s)=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,t,S{=}s]$
be the optimal subject-aware predictor with risk
$R_c=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-h^\star\|^2$. Then
\begin{equation}
  R_u-R_c=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_t,t}\!\big[\operatorname{Var}_{S}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,t,S]\,\big|\,\mathbf{x}_t,t\big)\big]\;\ge\;0.
  \label{eq:propcond}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{Var}_S(\,\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_t,t)$ denotes the total variance
(trace of the conditional covariance) of the vector-valued conditional mean.
\end{proposition}

The gap in~\eqref{eq:propcond} is exactly the \emph{between-subject variance} of
the optimal denoiser: subject-conditioning lowers the irreducible denoising error
precisely to the extent that the optimal denoising map varies across subjects. If
all subjects share the same conditional law ($\mathcal{D}_s\equiv\mathcal{D}$),
the gap is zero and conditioning cannot help; the larger the inter-subject
heterogeneity, the larger the potential gain. This formalizes the motivation for
a subject-aware mechanism and is consistent with the empirically uneven gains
across subject pairs (Section~\ref{sec:exp_results}).

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonality loss enforces decorrelation]
\label{prop:orth}
Let $\mathbf{Z}^{c},\mathbf{Z}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$ be the column-wise
mean-centered content and subject feature matrices over a batch of size $B$, and
let $\mathcal{L}_o=\|{\mathbf{Z}^{c}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{Z}^{s}\|_F^2$ as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lo}. Then
$\mathcal{L}_o=B^2\,\|\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\mathbf{z}^{c},\mathbf{z}^{s})\|_F^2$,
so $\mathcal{L}_o=0$ if and only if the empirical cross-covariance between the
content and subject features vanishes; minimizing $\mathcal{L}_o$ drives the two
representations toward (second-order) decorrelation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Domain-adaptation bound for the content representation; recalled from~\cite{bendavid2010theory}]
\label{prop:da}
Let $D_\theta$ map an input to its content representation, $f$ a downstream
hypothesis, and $P^{c}_S,P^{c}_T$ the content-representation distributions on a
source and a target subject. For binary hypotheses under $0/1$ loss,
\begin{equation}
  R_T(f\circ D_\theta)\;\le\;R_S(f\circ D_\theta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}\big(P^{c}_S,P^{c}_T\big)+\lambda,
  \label{eq:da}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}$ is the $\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}$-divergence
and $\lambda$ is the ideal joint risk (the multi-class case is analogous with an
appropriate discrepancy).
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textbf{How the design relates to Eq.~\eqref{eq:da} (heuristic).}
\saddpm{} is \emph{designed} to act on the divergence term: by routing
subject-specific variation into the individual branch and decorrelating it from
the content branch (Proposition~\ref{prop:orth}), the content representation is
encouraged toward subject-invariance, which can shrink
$d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(P^{c}_S,P^{c}_T)$ and thereby targets
cross-subject \emph{adaptation-space drift}~\cite{gheibi2024drift}. We stress this
is \emph{motivation, not a guarantee}: $\mathcal{L}_o$ enforces within-batch
content--subject decorrelation, a related but distinct quantity from the
cross-domain content divergence in~\eqref{eq:da}, and the term $\lambda$ is not
controlled by our objective.

\paragraph{From ceiling to practice.}
The benefit quantified in Proposition~\ref{prop:cond} is a population
(Bayes-optimal) ceiling: it describes the best achievable benefit of
conditioning, not what a finite network with learned embeddings attains. Realizing the gap
in~\eqref{eq:propcond} requires both embeddings $\mathbf{e}(s)$ that are
informative about $s$ and sufficient per-subject data to estimate $h^\star$. The
modest absolute gains reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp_results} indicate that only
a fraction of this ceiling is realized in practice, which we attribute to limited
data per subject and the difficulty of learning fully disentangled embeddings---a
gap that the future directions in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} aim to close.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_phase2} offers direct empirical support for this mechanism:
the subject embedding contributes materially when artifacts are
subject-specific---so the between-subject variance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:propcond} is
non-zero---and becomes nearly inert when artifacts are shared across subjects.

\subsection{Conditional Denoiser Variant (\mbox{SADDPM-Cond})}
\label{sec:method_cond}

The \saddpm{} model described so far is a \emph{generative restoration} denoiser:
it learns the target EEG distribution and denoises an observed segment through
partial noising followed by subject-conditioned reverse refinement. We
additionally study a \emph{conditional} variant, \mbox{SADDPM-Cond}, intended for the
paired setting in which (corrupted, clean) examples are available
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_phase2}). It shares the backbone, subject embedding, and
FiLM conditioning of \saddpm{}, with three changes.

\paragraph{Conditional input.} The corrupted observation $\mathbf{y}$ is
concatenated with the diffusion state along the channel axis, so the network sees
$[\mathbf{x}_t;\mathbf{y}]$ and learns $p(\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{y},s)$; training
uses synthetic pairs (a clean signal plus a known artifact at a controlled
signal-to-noise ratio).

\paragraph{$\mathbf{x}_0$-prediction.} Instead of predicting the noise
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, \mbox{SADDPM-Cond} predicts the clean signal $\mathbf{x}_0$
directly, which we found markedly more accurate for artifact removal
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_phase2}).

\paragraph{Full conditional generation.} At inference, \mbox{SADDPM-Cond} denoises by
\emph{full conditional generation}: the reverse process is run from pure Gaussian
noise while conditioning on $\mathbf{y}$ at every step, following the
image-to-image diffusion recipe of Palette and SR3~\cite{saharia2022palette,saharia2022sr3}.
The output is the denoised estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$.

\mbox{SADDPM-Cond} is therefore not a different model but the same
backbone, subject embedding, and FiLM conditioning operated in a
\emph{conditional-posterior} mode rather than a \emph{generative-prior} mode: it is
used only when paired (corrupted, clean) data are available, so that denoising
fidelity can be measured directly against a known clean target. Table~\ref{tab:variant}
contrasts the two along the axes that differ.

\subsection{A Unifying View: Conditioning Reduces Denoising Risk}
\label{sec:method_unify}

The benefit of subject conditioning  (Proposition~\ref{prop:cond}) is one instance
of a single principle that also governs the conditional and multi-channel settings of \mbox{SADDPM-Cond}: \emph{conditioning the Bayes-optimal denoiser on additional relevant information reduces its mean-squared error by exactly the corresponding conditional variance.} We record two further instances below (Propositions~\ref{prop:obs} and~\ref{prop:joint}); a supporting $x_0/\epsilon$ parameterization lemma and all proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{proposition}[Subject conditioning under an observed artifact]
\label{prop:obs}
Consider recovering a clean signal $\mathbf{x}_0$ from a corrupted observation
$\mathbf{y}$ (e.g., $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_0+\mathbf{a}$ with artifact
$\mathbf{a}$), with random subject $S$. Let the subject-agnostic and subject-aware Bayes denoisers be $\bar{g}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{y}]$ and
$\bar{h}(\mathbf{y},s)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{y},S{=}s]$, with risks
$\bar{R}_u,\bar{R}_c$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \bar{R}_u-\bar{R}_c=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\!\big[\operatorname{Var}_{S}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{y},S]\,\big|\,\mathbf{y}\big)\big]\ge 0,
  \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
which is zero iff the Bayes denoiser is subject-invariant ($\bar{h}(\mathbf{y},S)=\bar{g}(\mathbf{y})$ almost surely)---in particular when
$\mathbf{x}_0$ is recoverable from $\mathbf{y}$ alone (a \emph{shared}
artifact)---and strictly positive when the artifact is \emph{subject-specific}, so
that $\mathbf{y}$ under-determines $\mathbf{x}_0$ without $S$.
\end{proposition}

This is the theoretical counterpart of the functional-relevance result in Section~\ref{sec:exp_subject_loadbearing}: a shared artifact makes the embedding nearly inert ($\Delta\mathrm{CC}\approx 0$), whereas a subject-specific artifact makes the embedding materially useful.

\begin{proposition}[Joint versus per-channel denoising]
\label{prop:joint}
For channel $c$, let the per-channel Bayes denoiser use only that channel's observation, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0,c}\mid\mathbf{y}_c]$, and the joint denoiser use all channels, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0,c}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:C}]$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{c}\mathbb{E}\big\|\mathbf{x}_{0,c}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0,c}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:C}]\big\|^2
  \;\le\;
  \sum_{c}\mathbb{E}\big\|\mathbf{x}_{0,c}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0,c}\mid\mathbf{y}_c]\big\|^2,
  \label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}
with per-channel gap
$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}_c}\!\big[\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0,c}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:C}]\mid\mathbf{y}_c)\big]$,
which is strictly positive when the artifact has cross-channel (spatial) structure.
\end{proposition}

This explains why joint multi-channel denoising (Table~\ref{tab:p2_multi}, CC~$\approx 0.99$) exceeds per-channel baselines (CC~$\approx 0.85$): the joint denoiser exploits the artifact's spatial topography that per-channel models
discard.

The $x_0$- and $\epsilon$-parameterizations of the reverse mean are related by an
affine bijection and therefore share the same Bayes-optimal predictor, differing
only in the per-step weighting of the training loss (stated as
Lemma~\ref{lem:x0eps} in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}). Consequently, the empirical
advantage of $\mathbf{x}_0$-prediction (Table~\ref{tab:p2_single},
CC~$0.848\!\to\!0.901$) is an optimization and loss-weighting effect---an
effective emphasis on low-noise steps, where $\mathbf{x}_0$ is well
determined---rather than added model capacity.
