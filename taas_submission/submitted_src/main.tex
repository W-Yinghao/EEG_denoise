\begin{abstract}
Electroencephalography (EEG) is widely used in cognitive neuroscience, clinical monitoring, and brain--computer interfaces (BCIs), but its low signal-to-noise ratio and strong inter-subject variability make denoising hard to generalize across users. We propose a Subject-Aware Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (SADDPM): on top of the standard DDPM forward/reverse mechanism, it introduces learnable subject-embedding vectors injected at multiple network levels, so the reverse denoising trajectory adapts to each individual. Adaptation to a new subject is \emph{transductive} rather than zero-shot: only the subject embedding is estimated, from a small amount of \emph{unlabeled} target-subject EEG, with no task labels, clean targets, or artifact annotations. On BCI Competition~IV-2a, downstream motor-imagery classification and a subject-consistency analysis show SADDPM is more stable than traditional baselines across source--target pairs, with gains concentrated in cross-subject transfer and individual structure preserved. To assess fidelity directly, a conditional paired-data variant, \saddpmcond{}, is competitive with supervised denoisers on single-channel ocular-artifact removal, achieves high-fidelity joint multi-channel denoising, and shows functionally relevant conditioning when artifacts are subject-specific. These results support subject-aware diffusion as a viable adaptive framework for EEG denoising and BCI preprocessing.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Ethics and Privacy Statement}
This study uses only the publicly available, de-identified BCI Competition~IV-2a
dataset; no new human-subjects data were collected, and no personally
identifying information is used. EEG signals are sensitive physiological data,
and models trained on them could in principle be misused for individual
profiling; the subject-aware component here is intended solely to improve
cross-subject denoising for legitimate analysis and brain--computer interface
research, and the learned subject embeddings are not designed for biometric
identification of individuals. We see no additional societal risks beyond those
already considered by the original dataset's collection and release.
